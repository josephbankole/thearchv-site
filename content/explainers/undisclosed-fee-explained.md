Two clubs complete a transfer. Both announcements say the fee is undisclosed. Within an hour, three reporters have published a number.

Everybody involved knows the figure. The silence is a choice, and it is worth understanding whose.

## There is no rule

Start here, because it is the thing most people assume wrongly.

No English football regulation requires a club to publish a transfer fee. Clubs file accounts, and aggregate transfer spending appears there eventually, but the per-deal number is a matter of agreement between the two clubs. Undisclosed is not a compliance position. It is a decision.

## The four reasons

**To protect the next negotiation.** A published fee becomes a benchmark. Sell a 21-year-old central midfielder for £40m and every club selling a 21-year-old central midfielder for the next two years starts at £40m. A selling club with three more of them to trade has a direct interest in nobody knowing.

**To avoid pricing a player in public.** A fee becomes the frame every performance is judged against. A club signing a player it expects to develop slowly has a reason to keep the number out of the first sentence of every match report about him.

**Because the structure resists a single number.** A deal with a guaranteed fee, four tiers of add-ons, a sell-on percentage and payment across three years does not have one figure. It has a range. Announcing a point inside that range invites an argument about which point.

**Because one club wants it quiet.** A club selling cheaply, or buying at a price its own supporters would question, has an obvious motive. This is the least discussed reason and not the least common.

## How the number gets out anyway

Roughly four routes, and they explain why undisclosed rarely lasts.

Agents talk, because a large fee is an advertisement for the agent. Executives brief, because the direction of the leak flatters somebody. The accounts eventually show aggregate spend, which constrains what the individual deals can have been. And the other twenty clubs in the league find out through the ordinary traffic of people who all know each other.

What emerges is usually the guaranteed fee or the maximum, sometimes both, which is why reported figures for the same deal differ. That is a structure problem rather than a reliability problem.

## What this archive does with it

The standing rule here is to lead on the highest fee a named source printed, and never to invent one or round one up.

So an undisclosed deal is reported with the figure a named reporter has published, attributed to that reporter in the sentence, with the status label attached. Where two named reporters print different totals, both are given and the difference is explained rather than averaged.

Where nobody has printed a number, the fee is left out. An estimate presented as a fee is a fabrication with a decimal point, and Transfermarkt market values are estimates, so they never satisfy this rule on their own.

## The clubs that always disclose

Publicly listed clubs, and clubs in jurisdictions with stricter disclosure obligations, sometimes have to state material transactions. That is why a fee occasionally appears in a stock exchange filing before it appears in a newspaper.

It is a reminder that the secrecy is contractual rather than natural. Where somebody is obliged to publish, the number publishes fine.

## The one-line version

Undisclosed means the two clubs agreed not to say, almost always to protect a future negotiation, and almost never for long.
